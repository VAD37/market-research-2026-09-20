## Storytelling as a moat — tested, and it does not hold

Three cases, all against.

**Tome is the direct test.** A product named and branded around narrative. Founder's own words: *"We killed a product used by 25M+ people... Most founders used Tome's slide tool to tell their story."*

| Tome | Figure |
|---|---|
| Users | **20M in 18 months** (rip.so) vs **25M+** (founder's own post) — both on file |
| Raised | **$81.5M**, $300M valuation early 2024 |
| ARR | **Under $4M through 2024** |
| Outcome | Shut down 2025-04-30 |

Larger reach than Runway, Synthesia, HeyGen, Gamma or Captions in this pull. Stated causes: revenue never scaled, GPT-4 API costs exceeded subscription revenue, free-to-paid conversion failed. The other founder quote is blunter — the shutdown call came from *"none of us like the product."*

**Storytelling-as-brand drove adoption and not monetization.**

**Jasper is the marketing-without-moat case.** $125M at $1.5B post-money in 2022-10 — one month before ChatGPT launched. Peak ~$120M revenue in 2023, down to ~$55M by 2024 per one source, ~$35M per another (both on file, neither company-disclosed). Four layoff rounds, founders out of operating roles by 2023-09. The mechanism: buyers asked why they should pay $49/mo for what the OpenAI playground did free.

**Humane and Rabbit generalize it past software.** Humane: TED-talk launch, Sam Altman backing, $230M raised, **fewer than 10,000 units shipped**, returns outpacing sales by summer 2024, devices bricked 2025-02-28. Rabbit R1: 100,000 units sold off a CES demo, then mass returns when the demonstrated capabilities did not work; unpaid salaries reported by early 2026.

Four companies, four excellent narratives, four failures. A story sells the first cohort. Nothing in this pull shows it holding one.

## What creative AI work actually bills — the real numbers

Pass 2 carried an unattributed secondary claim of $3,000–$7,000/mo retainers and $20,000+/mo enterprise ContentOps. **Replace it.** The pricing unit was wrong: this work bills per deliverable, not per month.

| Tier | Price | Label |
|---|---|---|
| Raw model API | **$0.07–$0.40 per second** | vendor-reported |
| 30-second video, model fees only | **$2–$12 to generate. Once** | analyst-derived |
| Freelance AI video | **$500–$3,000 per video** | analyst-derived |
| Studio / agency | **from ~$5,000 per 30-second spot** | analyst-derived |
| DIY tool stack | $170–$685/mo in subscriptions | analyst-derived |
| Traditional national spot, for contrast | **$250,000–$500,000** | analyst-derived |

One case names both a client and a fee: **Kalshi's NBA Finals spot, ~$2,000 production cost**, documented by NPR, June 2025.

Generic video-editing retainers (not AI-specific) run $1,800–$5,000/mo: YouTube long-form 8 videos $2,500–5,000, Shorts 40 clips $1,800, podcast 16 episodes + 32 clips $5,000. Hourly bands $20–40 beginner, $45–75 mid, **$80–150 expert**, platform median ~$32–35. Third-party aggregation of Upwork postings — Upwork's own rate page returned 403.

**The $20,000+/mo enterprise ContentOps figure was not found anywhere.** Neither confirmed nor debunked. `unknown — checked three sources 2026-09-16`. Do not carry it forward.

Gap between model cost and sale price: **$2–$12 of compute against $500–$5,000 of invoice.** That margin is the business, and it is available to anyone who can direct the work.

| **USA** | **$1,500–3,000** | **$4,000–7,000** | **$8,000–20,000** |
| Canada | $1,200–2,500 | $3,500–6,000 | $7,000–15,000 |

Vendor-reported by an outsourcing studio — self-interested, treat the direction as solid and the precision as soft.

**Vietnamese mid-tier animation sells at $300–600/minute against $4,000–7,000 in the US. Roughly a 10x gap.** The team is in Ho Chi Minh City with an animator in it.

The qualifier that matters: this gap has existed since the late 1950s. Outsourcing to Japan, Korea, Taiwan, China, the Philippines, India and Vietnam is a seventy-year-old pattern. **AI is layered onto an already-outsourced labour market; it did not create the arbitrage and does not give a newcomer a claim on it.** Competing on that gap means competing with established studios that have held those relationships for decades.

## Animation labour, 2026 — and a reversal

The repo's previous data was dated 2024-04-11. It is now superseded, and the direction of the correction is a surprise.

**The 2024 forecast** (CVL Economics, commissioned by the Animation Guild, 2024-01): 204,000 US entertainment jobs disrupted by 2026, 62,000 in California; 21.4% of film/TV/animation jobs — ~118,500 — consolidated, replaced or eliminated by 2026; **29% of animation jobs identified as disruptable**; 78% of animation companies had adopted or planned to adopt AI.

**The 2026 finding contradicts it.** Otis College, reported by The Hollywood Reporter, 2026-04: *"The jobs disappeared, but the economic data says AI did not cause it."* Researchers found the pattern of job loss *"does not support the fact that there's been displacement of workers by AI."* And the detail that lands hardest: **"The roles most exposed to AI, including writers, software developers and artists, grew in number."**

The jobs did go. The cause was the streaming pullback and production contraction:

- California's creative economy lost **114,000 jobs 2022–2025, down 14%.** Film, TV and sound employment fell **30%.**
- **One third of the Animation Guild's working members were laid off in a single year** (union disclosure, 2024-07).
- Disney ~1,000 (2026-04, Marvel's visual development team nearly eliminated). Pixar 175 — **14% of the workforce** (2024). Netflix feature animation ~50, one third of the division (2023-11). DreamWorks ~70 (2023-10).
- LA shoot days **19,694 in 2025, down 16.1%.** Global kids TV commissions down **18%** April 2025–March 2026.

US animator employment now: **19,970 wage-and-salary jobs**, 57,100 including self-employed, **61.5% self-employed** (BLS). Median wage **$102,030**. BLS projects 2% growth to 2034 — about 900 jobs — with self-employment falling 3.4% while salaried rises 9.6%.

Japan for contrast: mean animator income **¥4.446M**, median ¥4.0M; entry-level in-between animators **¥2.275M (~US$15,000)** per the Japan Animation Creators Association, 2026-03.

**Read:** the animation labour market contracted hard and AI is not why. That is better news for an animator than the 2024 forecast was, and it removes "AI is taking animation jobs" as a premise for anything built here.

## The union contract — E8 closed

**Ratified.** The Animation Guild's 2024–2027 Master CBA: MOA signed **2024-08-01**, ratified **December 2024 at 76.1%**, running through July 2027. First negotiated AI provisions for unionised animation workers in the US.

What it secured: written notice when AI will be used on covered work; consultation rights and identification of alternative tools; studios must hand over written AI policies on request. Also a minimum of three writers for animated TV shows, with exceptions.

What it did not: **members cannot prevent their work from training AI models, and cannot opt out if AI use is required for their role.** The union withdrew its AI training opt-out proposals "after meeting strong resistance." Members had demanded an outright ban on AI in animation; they did not get one. Negotiation was constrained by a pre-existing clause allowing "technological changes."

Notice and transparency, no control. `unknown`: the signed MOA PDF could not be read — no PDF extraction available to that agent — so every clause above is trade-press paraphrase, not the union's own words. The verbatim text remains unpulled.

## Sora's 1,600x conflict — resolved

Pass 2 recorded Sora's revenue as **$3.4B/yr** in one source and **$2.1M/yr** in another and called it unresolvable.

It was a category error in the secondary source. **$3.4B is OpenAI's whole-company annualized revenue** (mid-2024, The Information). **$2.1M is the Sora app's own lifetime revenue** through its March 2026 shutdown (WSJ-sourced). Different entities, different time bases. No contradiction.

Sora's real number is $2.1M lifetime. Against a reported ~$15M/day run cost, that is the scale of the failure.
